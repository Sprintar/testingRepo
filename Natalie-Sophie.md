Natalie didn’t plan to stay long at the community “Repair Saturday.” She just wanted her desk lamp to work again so she could stop typing in the dark after work.

The gym smelled like coffee and warm plastic. Volunteers sat at folding tables, each with a little sign: Bikes, Clothing, Small Electronics. Natalie queued under “Electronics,” holding the lamp and a hopeful expression.

“Next!” called a woman in a navy hoodie. She had a tidy ponytail, steady hands, and a name tag that read Sophie (Volunteer).

Natalie set the lamp down. “It flickers. I’ve tried a new bulb. Still flickers.”

Sophie turned the lamp over and clicked her tongue. “Loose switch, probably. Mind if I open it?”

“Go for it.”

While Sophie worked, Natalie watched the small, careful movements: screws in a neat row, cord draped out of the way, the tiny click of a multimeter probe. No theatrics—just patience.

“So,” Sophie said, eyes on the switch, “what’s the lamp for?”

“Late-night emails,” Natalie said. “I’m new in town. New job. Too many passwords to learn.”

Sophie smiled. “I can’t help with the passwords, but I can fix this.” She nudged a wire, tightened something, tested again. The bulb glowed steady. “There.”

Natalie blinked at the light. “You just saved my eyesight.”

“Happy to serve.” Sophie reassembled the base. “You can test it at the outlet over there, if you want.”

They carried the lamp to the wall. The light came on—solid, unwavering.

Sophie crossed her arms, satisfied. “Victory.”

“Let me repay you with coffee,” Natalie said, surprising herself a little. “There’s a stand by the door.”

“You don’t have to,” Sophie said, then added, “but I won’t stop you.”

They took two paper cups to a quiet corner. The conversation stayed easy: where they’d grown up, which streets were fastest at rush hour, the trick for finding fresh bread after 6 p.m. Sophie mentioned she volunteered every month, and sometimes built small gadgets at home “for fun and to avoid cleaning.”

Natalie laughed. “I cook to avoid cleaning. Maybe we can trade: you fix things, I feed us.”

“That sounds dangerously efficient,” Sophie said. “I’m in.”

When the cups were empty, Natalie picked up her now-reliable lamp. “Thanks again.”

“Anytime,” Sophie said, tapping the table with a fingertip. “See you around?”

“Yeah,” Natalie said, meaning it. “See you around.”

On her way out, Natalie glanced back. Sophie was already helping the next person, the same calm focus on her face. The lamp felt warm in Natalie’s hand—steady light, simple fix, and a new name saved in her phone.
